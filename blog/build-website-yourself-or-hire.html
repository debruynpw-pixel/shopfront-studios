Build It Yourself or Hire Someone? An Honest Answer

By PW de Bruyn 4 August 2026 8 min read

We build websites for a living, so treat what follows with appropriate suspicion — but the honest position is that DIY builders have become good enough that plenty of South African businesses should use them. The failure mode is almost never the software. It is that a website takes thirty-odd hours to do properly, and thirty hours of an owner's time is the most expensive resource in a small business.

Short answer

Build it yourself if the site is simple, your budget is tight, and you have twenty to forty uninterrupted hours you are willing to spend. Hire someone if your time is worth more than the build costs, if the site has to sell rather than merely exist, or if you have already started once and stalled.

What does DIY actually involve?

The adverts show someone dragging a photo onto a page and smiling. The real work is in a different place entirely.

Choosing a platform takes an evening. Picking a template takes another. Neither is hard. Then you hit the part nobody advertises: writing the words. Eight pages describing your own business, in a way that sounds like a person and answers what a customer wants to know, is genuinely difficult — and it is where the overwhelming majority of half-finished DIY sites stop. Not at the design. At the About page.

After that comes photographs. Stock images of models in hard hats undermine you instantly; customers can tell. Real photos of your work, your team and your vehicles are worth more than any template, and taking usable ones takes an afternoon.

Then structure: which pages exist, what each is for, what a visitor should do next. This is the part that decides whether the site produces enquiries, and it is invisible — no builder prompts you to think about it.

Finally the plumbing: connecting your domain, making the contact form actually deliver to your inbox, adding a WhatsApp link, setting up Google Business Profile, checking it on a real phone. Each is small. Together they are a day.

Realistically, a determined owner produces a decent five-page site in twenty to forty hours spread over a few weeks. The people who succeed are the ones who accept that number upfront. The ones who budget "a weekend" are the ones with a half-built site still sitting there in March.

What does each route really cost?

Compare total cost over three years, not the sticker price, and count your own hours as real.

Time to liveWeeks, depends on you2–6 weeks
CopywritingYouUsually included

Two things people miss. First, the subscription never stops — over several years a monthly plan quietly adds up to a meaningful figure, and if you stop paying, the site goes away. A once-off build with cheap hosting has a different shape: more upfront, less forever. Neither is universally cheaper; it depends how long the site lives. The bands are in our breakdown of what a website costs in South Africa.

Second, your hours have a price. If thirty hours of your attention would otherwise go into quoting jobs or serving customers, the DIY route is not free. For a tradesperson who bills their day, it is frequently the more expensive option in pure rands — which is not an argument, it is arithmetic you can do yourself.

When is DIY the right call?

You are testing whether the business works. Pre-revenue, still deciding what you sell. Spending real money on a site before you know what goes on it is premature. Build something simple, learn, replace it later.

The site only needs to exist. Some businesses genuinely need a credible page confirming they are real, with contact details and a few photos. If that is the job, a builder does it well.

You enjoy it. Not a small factor. Owners who find it satisfying keep the site current, and a maintained simple site beats a neglected sophisticated one.

You need to change things constantly. Weekly specials, changing stock, a menu that moves. Doing that yourself, immediately, is worth more than a designer's polish.

Cash is genuinely tight. A live DIY site beats a designer quote you cannot accept. Something honest and working today is better than perfect and hypothetical.

If you go this route, spend your effort in the right places: real photographs, plain writing that answers actual customer questions, a phone number and WhatsApp link on every single page, and a fully completed Google Business Profile. That last one will do more for a local business than any amount of template polish.

When does DIY cost you more than hiring?

When the site has to sell. There is a real difference between a page that describes your services and a page built so that a specific visitor takes a specific action. That difference is the whole job — knowing what to put first, what to cut, and where the button goes. It is what we mean when we talk about the setup that actually gets plumbers called, and it is not something a template teaches you.

When you are in a competitive category. If five other businesses in your town all have decent sites, "existing" is not enough. Someone has to think about why a visitor picks you — which is a positioning problem before it is a design one.

When your time is genuinely scarce. If you are turning down work to build a website, stop. That is the clearest signal in this entire article.

When you have already started and stalled. A half-built site sitting untouched for two months is not a budget decision anymore, it is a bottleneck. Hire out the part you are stuck on — often just the writing.

When the build needs anything unusual. Bookings tied to specific staff, quote calculators, document uploads, integrations with your existing systems. Builders handle browse-and-contact well and bespoke flows badly, as we set out in custom vs template.

When professional appearance is the product. Attorneys, accountants, medical practices, anyone selling expertise and trust. A slightly-off site quietly undermines the thing you are selling, which is precisely why we keep a dedicated page for attorneys rather than treating every business the same.

Is there a middle option?

Several, and they are underused.

Do the site, buy the words. Build it yourself, pay a copywriter for the four pages that matter. The writing is the hard part and the part that converts. This is the best value option on this list for most owners.

Pay for the structure, build the rest. Some designers will do a paid consultation — page structure, what goes where, what to say — and let you execute it. A few hours of someone else's judgement removes the guesswork that sinks most DIY attempts.

Start DIY, rebuild on evidence. Launch something honest now. In six months you will know which pages people visit and which questions keep coming up. Handing that to a designer produces a far better second site than any amount of upfront speculation.

Hire the build, keep the keys. Have someone build it properly, but insist on admin access so you can make small changes yourself. Best of both, and entirely normal to ask for.

Whichever route you take, do one thing on day one: register the domain in your own name. It costs very little, takes ten minutes, and it means every future decision stays yours — whether that is moving off a builder, changing designers, or rebuilding from scratch. Frequently asked questions

Can I really build my own business website?

Yes. Modern builders like Wix, Squarespace and Shopify are genuinely capable, and a determined owner can produce a decent five-page site. The constraint is rarely the software. It is the writing, the photographs, and the twenty to forty hours it realistically takes to finish rather than abandon.

Is a DIY website cheaper than hiring a designer?

Cheaper in rands, not always in total cost. You pay a monthly subscription instead of a once-off build, and you pay with your own time. If your working hours are worth anything to your business, thirty hours of building is a real cost that never appears on an invoice.

Is a Wix or Squarespace site bad for Google rankings?

No. Both are capable of ranking perfectly well, and the platform is rarely the reason a site does not appear. Far more common causes are thin pages that do not match what people search for, a missing Google Business Profile, and a site nobody links to or visits.

Can I start with a DIY site and hire someone later?

Yes, and it is often the sensible order. Build something simple, learn which pages people actually use and which questions customers keep asking, then hand that evidence to a designer. Just register the domain in your own name from day one so moving costs you nothing but the build.
