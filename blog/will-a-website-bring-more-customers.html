Will a New Website Actually Bring You More Customers?

By PW de Bruyn 4 August 2026 8 min read

This is the question underneath every website conversation, and most agencies answer it with a yes and a case study. The honest answer is more useful: a website almost never creates demand. It captures demand that already exists and would otherwise have gone somewhere else. Whether that is worth the money depends entirely on how much demand is currently leaking, which is a number you can estimate before you spend anything.

Short answer

A website brings customers only if something sends people to it and the page makes the next step obvious. On its own it is a brochure. Paired with Google Business Profile, referrals or ads, it converts people who were already looking for what you sell — which is where nearly all its value comes from.

What does a website actually do for a business?

Three jobs, and it is worth being precise about which one you need, because they call for different sites.

It closes referrals. Someone recommends you. The next thing that person does — nearly always — is look you up. What they find decides whether they call. This is the highest-value job a small business site does, and the one almost nobody measures, because those customers say "a friend recommended you" and the website silently gets no credit for keeping them.

It captures search. People who do not know you exist type what they need into Google. If you appear and the page answers them, you get a customer you would never otherwise have met. This is genuine new demand, but it is slow to build and depends on your category.

It removes friction. Prices, areas served, hours, whether you do that specific thing. Every question your site answers is a phone call you do not take and a customer who does not drift to a competitor because they could not be bothered to ask.

Notice what is not on the list: making people want your service. A website does not make anyone need an electrician. It decides which electrician they call.

Where would the visitors come from?

This is the question that predicts success, and it is the one that gets skipped. A beautiful site with no traffic source produces nothing — and that outcome is far more common than a bad site being the problem.

How do you work out if it pays for itself?

You can do this on the back of an envelope before spending anything, and it beats any agency projection.

Start with what a customer is worth. Not one job — the whole relationship. A plumber's average call-out is modest; a plumber's average customer, who calls again and recommends you, is worth several times that. Use the honest lifetime figure.

Then ask how many extra customers pay for the build. If a customer is worth R2,500 to you and a site costs R20,000, you need eight over its life. A website that lasts three years needs fewer than three extra customers a year. Framed that way, most service businesses clear the bar comfortably — and if yours doesn't, that is genuinely useful to know before you spend.

Then sanity-check the leak. Ask yourself how many people in the last year looked you up and found nothing, an outdated page, or a Facebook page they could not get a price from. You will not have a precise number. You do not need one — you need to know whether it is closer to two or two hundred.

A caution on the arithmetic: this tells you whether the investment is plausible, not what will happen. Anyone quoting you a specific percentage increase in enquiries from a website is guessing, and the honest version of this industry admits that. What you can control is whether the site captures the demand that reaches it. What you cannot control is how much demand exists.

If the numbers are marginal, the right answer may be to start smaller. Our breakdown of what websites cost in South Africa covers what each band buys, and the comparison with a Facebook page covers the cases where waiting is genuinely the right call.

Why do most websites produce nothing?

Four reasons, in order of how often they are the actual cause.

1. Nothing sends traffic to it. The site was built, launched, and then treated as finished. No Google Business Profile, no ads, no links, nothing. This is the cause in most cases and it has nothing to do with the site itself.

2. There is no obvious next action. A visitor arrives ready to enquire and has to hunt for how. The phone number is an image in the footer; the form asks for eleven fields; there is no WhatsApp button even though every one of your customers uses WhatsApp. This is the most fixable failure on the list and the most common after traffic — we take it apart in the setup that actually gets plumbers called.

3. The page does not answer the question the visitor arrived with. Someone searching for a specific service in a specific suburb lands on a generic home page about your company values. They leave. Matching the page to the intent is why we keep separate pages for each trade we work with, like attorneys and salons — the visitor should recognise themselves within one screen.

4. It is slow or awkward on a phone. Most South African visitors arrive on mobile, often on mobile data, sometimes on a mid-range Android. A site that takes eight seconds to appear loses people before they have seen anything you wrote.

What is rarely the cause, despite being what everyone worries about: the colour scheme, the logo, or whether the design looks modern enough. Those matter for credibility, and credibility matters — but they are almost never the reason a site produces zero enquiries.

How do you tell whether yours is working?

Count enquiries, not visitors. Traffic is a vanity number. The only figures that matter are how many people contacted you and what that was worth.

Ask every new customer how they found you. Unfashionable, free, and more accurate than most analytics setups for a small business. Write it in a notebook for three months and you will know more about your marketing than any dashboard would tell you.

Use a separate number or a tagged WhatsApp link if you can. The hardest thing to measure in South Africa is the WhatsApp enquiry, because it arrives looking identical to every other message. A distinct link from the site, or simply asking "did you find us on Google?", closes that gap.

Give it a fair window. Referral conversions show up immediately. Search takes months. Judging a new site after three weeks tells you nothing except whether it is broken.

Check the boring things quarterly. Does the contact form still deliver? Is the phone number current? Are your hours right for December? Forms fail silently and nobody finds out for months — which is a real cost, and an avoidable one.

If you are about to commission a site, the single most useful thing you can do is decide now what "working" will mean — a number of enquiries a month, say — and tell your designer that is the goal. It changes what gets built. A designer aiming at enquiries makes different decisions from one aiming at a nice-looking home page, and the list of questions in what to ask before paying a deposit is how you find out which one you are dealing with.

Frequently asked questions

Will a website bring me customers on its own?

No. A website captures demand that already exists — people searching for what you do, or checking you out after a referral. Something has to send visitors to it: Google Business Profile, search rankings, ads, social, or word of mouth. A site with no traffic source is a business card nobody has been handed.

How long before a new website starts producing enquiries?

Referral traffic converts immediately, because those people were already coming to check you out. Search traffic takes longer — typically three to six months for a new site to build any meaningful ranking, and longer in competitive categories. If you need enquiries this month, pair the site with Google Business Profile or paid ads.

How do I know if my website is working?

Count enquiries, not visitors. Set up call tracking or simply ask every new customer how they found you, then compare that against what the site cost. Traffic numbers with no enquiry number attached tell you almost nothing about whether the investment paid for itself.

What is the most common reason a website produces no leads?

No traffic source, followed closely by no obvious next action. A site that nobody visits cannot convert, and a site where the phone number is buried in a footer wastes the visitors it does get. Both are far more common causes than anything to do with how the site looks.
