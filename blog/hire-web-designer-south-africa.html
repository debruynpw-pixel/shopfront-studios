How to Hire a Web Designer in South Africa

By PW de Bruyn 4 August 2026 8 min read

Hiring a web designer is one of those purchases where you cannot judge the product before you buy it, cannot easily judge it after, and only find out whether it was any good about six months later when you look at where your enquiries came from. This guide is the process we would give a friend: what to prepare, where to look, what to check, and what actually separates a build that earns its money from one that just sits there.

Short answer

Write a one-page brief stating what the website must achieve, then approach three designers with that identical brief. Judge them on the questions they ask you, insist that domain, hosting and files are registered in your name, and get scope, revisions, timeline and post-launch support in writing before paying a deposit.

What should you decide before you contact anyone?

The most expensive websites in South Africa are not the ones with the biggest invoices. They are the ones that get rebuilt eighteen months later because nobody agreed at the start what the thing was for.

Before you speak to a designer, get clear on one sentence: what does this website need to make happen? For most small businesses the honest answer is one of three — get the phone to ring, get a quote request submitted, or get someone to book a slot. "Look professional" is a side effect of doing one of those well, not a goal in itself.

Then write down, roughly:

Who the visitor is and what state they arrive in. A burst geyser at 06:00 is a different visitor from someone comparing wedding venues over three weeks. The first needs a phone number above the fold and nothing else in the way. The second needs photographs and detail.

Where you want enquiries to land. Email, WhatsApp, a form, a call — pick, and say so. In South Africa the honest answer is usually WhatsApp, and a site built around that assumption converts noticeably better than one that hides a contact form on page four.

What you already have. Logo files, photographs, existing domain, existing hosting, a Google Business Profile. Knowing this stops you paying twice for things you own.

You do not need to specify a design style, choose a colour palette, or write the copy. Those are the designer's job, and a designer who asks you to supply all three is quoting for a layout rather than a website.

Where do South African businesses find web designers?

There are four realistic routes, and they are not equal.

Referral from another business owner. Consistently the best source, for the boring reason that you get to see a finished site and interrogate someone who lived through the process. Ask specifically whether the designer replied quickly after the invoice was paid.

Google search. Searching "web designer" plus your town surfaces people who at minimum can rank their own site, which is a live demonstration of a skill you are buying. Look past the ads to who holds the map pack.

Sites you admire. If you like a South African business's website, scroll to the footer — the builder is often credited. This is the most under-used method available.

Freelance marketplaces. Upwork, Fiverr and the directory sites do work, and the price can be very low. The trade-offs are real, though: little accountability once the milestone is released, frequent time-zone friction, and a strong tendency toward template output. If the site is central to how you get customers, this is the riskiest of the four.

Wherever you look, favour someone who has built for a business shaped like yours. It is why we keep separate pages for the trades and professions we work with most — an electrician's site and an attorney's site are solving genuinely different problems, and it shows in the first conversation.

Freelancer, studio or agency — which suits you?

There is no universally correct answer, only a fit question. The table below is a fair summary of the trade-offs as they play out in the South African market.

For a plumber, salon, accountant or law firm, a solo designer or small studio is usually the right call — and the premium you would pay an agency buys coordination you do not need. Where an agency genuinely earns it is when several specialists must work simultaneously: an e-commerce build with a stock system, or a brand rollout across print and web at once.

The freelancer risk is worth naming honestly. It is not skill. It is that one person can get sick, take a full-time job, or take on too much. Mitigate it the boring way — own your domain and hosting, and get the files. How do you check someone is worth hiring?

Six checks, in rough order of how much they tell you.

1. Judge the questions, not the pitch. The designer who asks what a customer is worth to you, where your work currently comes from, and what you want someone to do on the site will build you something different from the one who asks how many pages you want. This is the single best predictor available before money changes hands.

2. Open their portfolio on your phone. Not on a laptop. Load two or three of their live sites on mobile data and count the seconds. If their own client sites are slow and fiddly on a mid-range Android, yours will be too.

3. Phone a client from last year. Ask for two whose sites went live more than six months ago. Recent clients are still delighted. Six-month clients know whether messages got answered.

4. Get ownership in writing. Domain in your name, hosting you can access, files handed over on final payment. This is the most common way South African businesses end up trapped, and it is entirely preventable with one email.

5. Read the quote for what is missing. Copy, photography, year-two hosting and post-launch support are the four usual absences. Our guide to comparing website quotes walks through normalising three quotes so they describe the same job.

What does the process look like once you've hired?

A normal small-business build runs roughly like this: brief and deposit, then a week or two of structure and copy, then design, then build, then a review round, then launch. Two to six weeks is typical for five to eight pages.

The part nobody warns you about is that you are usually the bottleneck. Projects stall waiting for photos, prices, and sign-off on wording — not on the designer. If you want it fast, block out two hours in week one to answer questions properly, and give one person in your business the authority to approve.

Ask before launch what happens to your Google Business Profile and existing search rankings, especially if you are replacing an old site. URLs that change without redirects lose whatever visibility they had, and recovering it takes months. Our Google Business Profile guide covers the local search side of that switchover.

And agree the day-after arrangement in advance: who fixes what, how fast, and what a small change costs in a year. A website is a piece of equipment, not a painting. Budget for it accordingly and it will keep working.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to hire a web designer in my own city?

No. Almost all South African web work is now done remotely, and a designer in Cape Town can build for a business in Polokwane without either party travelling. What matters is that they understand South African customers — ZAR pricing, WhatsApp enquiries, mobile data, and local search — not that they are down the road.

Is it better to hire a freelancer or a web design agency?

For a small service business, a solo designer or a two-person studio is usually the better fit: you speak to the person doing the work and decisions happen the same day. Agencies earn their premium on larger builds needing several specialists at once. The real risk with a freelancer is availability, not skill.

What do I need to prepare before hiring a web designer?

Three things: a one-page brief saying what the site must achieve, a list of the pages you think you need, and your logo and any photos you already own. You do not need to write the copy or choose a design style. A good designer expects to lead both of those.

How long does it take to build a small business website in South Africa?

A straightforward five to eight page site typically takes two to six weeks from deposit to launch. The build itself is rarely the slow part. Projects usually stall waiting for content from the client — photos, prices, and approval on wording — so the timeline you are quoted assumes you reply promptly.

Start with the brief, not the price.
